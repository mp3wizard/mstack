### Interview study

**You own the study kit and the synthesis. A human recruits and runs every interview. Never simulate a participant, a quote, or a theme, even labeled as simulated (principle-never-fabricate-user-evidence).**

Generative tier 3 research. Use it when the question is about needs, circumstances, or current behavior, usually before a design exists. Whether people can use a design goes to **Usability test** (playbooks/usability-test.md). Prepare the kit, hand it over, record `pending: user evidence for <question>` in the todo list, and keep working on tiers 1 and 2.

1. Write the research questions. Name the decision the study informs, such as which problem to pursue or whether it is real, and the finding that would change it (**principle-frame-the-problem**). Draft the plan with design:user-research if installed, then check it against these steps.
2. Write the recruit spec. One screener per distinct group, selecting on recent behavior, such as who did the task in a recent window, never on stated interest. Include people who tried and left or never adopted. Include disabled and assistive-technology users. Plan small rounds and add one while new interviews still change the themes.
3. Write the consent and data-handling note. What is recorded, who sees it, how long it is kept, and how to withdraw. Add the compensation plan. Follow the 18F recruiting, privacy, and compensation cards where they fit.
4. Write the discussion guide. Open with the participant's context. Then ask for one specific recent instance, such as "Tell me about the last time you...", and follow it in order with "what happened next" and "why then". For a switch or adoption question, rebuild the timeline from first thought to first use, and probe what pushed them, what pulled them, what worried them, and what held them back. No preference questions, no "would you use", no feature pitches, and no leading prompts (**principle-observe-behavior-over-opinion**).
5. Write the note template. One sheet per session with a participant code and no name, the group, the story in sequence, verbatim quotes with timestamps, workarounds and tools seen, and open questions. Keep what the person said apart from the note-taker's reading of it.
6. Write the synthesis template. Code each note, cluster the codes into themes by affinity or thematic analysis, and report each theme with n of N participants, quotes by participant code, and counter-evidence. A theme from one participant is an anecdote. Map themes to the problem statement or the opportunities it opens.
7. Review the guide yourself, prompt by prompt, as the evaluator. Check that each asks about past behavior, gives no hint, and fits the session. Never answer it in a participant's voice. Ask the human to pilot it with one colleague.
8. Hand the kit over and keep working. Contacting participants is outward, so a human does it (see Autonomy in the router). Label everything you produce meanwhile with its tier.
9. When a human returns real notes or recordings, remove names and identifying details before analysis, and keep raw recordings only where the consent allows. Synthesize with design:research-synthesis if installed, else per the step 6 template, and check its output against these steps. Never extend a theme past what participants said or did.
10. Write the study report. The answer to each research question, the themes with n of N, what the findings change, and the next questions. Pause before sharing the report outside the session (see Autonomy in the router).

**Reply:** the research questions, the kit paths, the pending gate, and after a round the themes with n of N, the answer to each research question, and the next questions.

**Grounded in:** [GOV.UK Service Manual: User research](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research) (OGL v3.0, adapted), [Continuous Discovery Habits](https://www.producttalk.org/2021/08/continuous-discovery/) (Teresa Torres, © Product Talk, no open license found, paraphrased) for story-based interviews about a specific past instance, [Jobs to Be Done](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/theory/jobs-to-be-done/) (Christensen Institute, all rights reserved, paraphrased) for needs as progress in a circumstance, [18F Methods](https://github.com/18F/guides/tree/main/content/methods) (CC0) for the user interview, affinity mapping, qualitative data analysis, recruiting, privacy, and compensation cards.
